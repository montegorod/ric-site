How to read a trading drawdown, and when it is normal

A drawdown is the distance between the account's peak and its current value. Every system that trades has them, and a drawdown by itself does not mean something is broken. Trouble starts not when a drawdown appears, but when it is not understood: when nobody knows how deep the account can go, how long that may last, or how this drawdown differs from the previous ones.

What to look at instead of the drawdown size

The percentage on its own is useless: for an account risking 1% per trade and one risking 10%, "a 15% drawdown" means completely different things. Look at four things:

  1. Depth. How far the account has moved from its peak. The useful part is not the number but the comparison with previous drawdowns: if the worst used to be 8% and is now 20%, what changed is the system's behaviour, not the market.
  2. Duration. How long the account has been below its peak. A month in drawdown while a strategy works is normal and expected. Six months is a reason to ask questions even if the depth is modest.
  3. Recovery speed. How quickly the account recovers after reaching the bottom. If every new drawdown takes longer to recover than the last, that is a trend, not a coincidence.
  4. The losing streak. How many consecutive losing trades the system survived inside this drawdown. This is tied directly to risk per trade, not to forecasting.

Floating and closed drawdowns are different numbers

This is the most common misunderstanding. A floating drawdown is the loss on open positions, and it can still change: it may come back into profit, close at break-even, or grow. A closed drawdown is a realised result that can no longer change.

The practical consequence: while an account shows a floating drawdown, you cannot compare it with a maximum allowed closed drawdown — they are different quantities. Conversely, if a system closes trades forcibly, the floating drawdown is capped by design.

When a drawdown is a warning sign

Signals worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as "the market being the market":

  • depth grows from drawdown to drawdown — usually a rise in risk per trade or a change in the system's logic;
  • recovery time increases — the strategy no longer fits the market;
  • the system does not return to its previous peak for substantially longer than in earlier cases;
  • open positions sit for weeks or months without closing — then the drawdown is structural, not temporary;
  • the drawdown is not capped: there is no rule that closes a losing position.

Why depth depends on risk per trade, not on luck

A drawdown is built from a series of losing trades, and its depth is set by how much of the account each trade loses. Two systems with an identical set of trades produce different drawdowns if one risks 1% per trade and the other 10%.

An example: ten consecutive losses at 1% risk produce roughly −10% of the account. At 10% risk the same series is about −65%, and at 20% risk it is close to losing the account entirely. The trades are the same; only position size differs.

So the right way to ask "what is this system's drawdown" is: what is the risk per trade, and how many consecutive losses does it survive.

How to check a system through its monitoring

Monitoring shows an account's results, but you should not look at a single number:

  1. Open the equity curve, not just the total return — the shape says more than the figure.
  2. Find the maximum drawdown and see how long the account took to recover from it.
  3. Compare the current drawdown with the historical ones: is it deeper, is it longer.
  4. Check whether any positions are open and how long they have been held.
  5. Establish which drawdowns are closed and which are floating.

If these questions have no answers, you are looking at a shop window rather than at results.
