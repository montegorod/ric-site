What a drawdown means for your money — in plain language

You will see this word in every honest report, and almost never in the dishonest ones. It deserves a plain definition, because it is the number that will actually affect how you sleep, far more than any return figure.

A drawdown is the distance from the highest point your balance has reached to the lower point where it stands now, expressed as a share of that high point.

An example with invented numbers, purely to show the arithmetic: a balance of $10,000 grows to $12,000, then falls to $10,200. The drawdown from the peak is 15%, even though the account is still above where it started. A drawdown can happen while you are still in profit — that is the part most people miss.

Why it happens, and why it is not a malfunction

Any strategy that takes risk will have losing trades; that is not a flaw, it is the mechanism. Losing trades arrive in clusters by chance, so a system can be right more often than not and still close a bad week, and occasionally a bad month.

Nothing is broken when the balance falls. Something is broken when the risk per trade is uncontrolled, or when the manager hides the fall. A drawdown is information. Its absence in a report is a warning sign, not a feature.

What limiting risk per trade changes — and what it does not

The maximum loss built into a single trade is small and set in advance, and it does not change with mood or with the previous trade. A losing streak therefore has a floor: even a run of bad trades does not push the account past a known limit, and one trade cannot empty it.

What that does not change:

  • The depth of a bad period. Risk control caps a single trade, not a month. A drawdown can still be uncomfortable.
  • Gaps and slippage. A market gap can produce a worse result on one trade than the planned maximum.
  • Profitability. Limiting losses does not create an edge. A strategy that makes no money is still that, with or without perfect risk control.

Getting back up takes longer than falling

This is arithmetic, and it is the reason depth matters so much. A balance that falls 20% needs about 25% on what remains to return to its old high. A balance that falls 50% needs 100% to get back to the same place. Losses and gains are not symmetrical, and the deeper the fall, the more work it takes to undo.

The time to recover cannot be known in advance. An account can spend months below its previous high, and that stretch is part of the experience of investing this way, not an exception to it.

What that means for money you might need soon

If you need the money on a particular date, a drawdown gives you two bad options: wait, or withdraw at the low point and make the loss permanent. That is why money you need within a year does not belong in a trading account — not because the strategy is bad, but because your calendar and the market do not negotiate.

What you should be able to see from anyone you trust with money

  • How deep the fall from the high point has gone in the real history, and how long the recovery took.
  • The losing days and losing periods, published without being tidied up.
  • Live monitoring of a real account, so the curve can be read by you rather than described to you.
