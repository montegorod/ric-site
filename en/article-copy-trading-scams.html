Copy trading reviews and scams: how to check a trader before you connect

Search for "copy trading reviews" and you get two extremes: raves like "doubled my deposit in a month" and cries of "scam, lost everything". The truth is usually more boring. Copy trading as a method is honest: the trader's trades are simply repeated on your account. It is not copy trading that cheats people, but the people around it. Below: the most common schemes, why you shouldn't take reviews at face value, and how to check any trader in 15 minutes — including us.

Where the scams really are

Almost every "copy trading is a scam" story comes down to four schemes.

"Send the money to me, I'll do everything." That is no longer copy trading. In copy trading the money sits in your own broker account, opened in your name, and the trader cannot withdraw it. If you are asked to send money to a card, a wallet or a "pooled account", there is nothing more to discuss.

Guaranteed income. "10% a month, every month", "no losses", "your deposit is protected". Markets don't work that way: every real trader has losing weeks and months. A guarantee of income is a sign not of confidence, but that what you are being sold is not trading.

Nice numbers without a verifiable account. Profitable screenshots are easy to fake, and even easier to cherry-pick: ten good trades out of a hundred. A result you cannot check is worth nothing.

Trading without stop-losses. The most treacherous scheme, because it can look great for months. The trader never closes losing trades and waits for the price to come back. It almost always does — and the equity curve rises in a smooth line. Until one day the price doesn't come back, and the account is wiped out in a day. Many "lost everything" reviews come from exactly this.

Why you shouldn't take reviews at face value

Reviews are useful, but only as a supplement. Glowing reviews can be paid for; angry ones often come from people who picked the most aggressive model in a bad month and quit at a loss. Both are honest in their own way, but they say little about the trader.

Look for something else in reviews: how long people have been with the trader, whether they mention withdrawals, whether they mention losing periods. A review like "we were down in March and recovered in April" tells you more about a trader than ten "everything's great".

How to check a trader in 15 minutes: a checklist

  1. A live account monitor. Ask for a link to a public monitor (services like Myfxbook or FXmonitor) where every trade is visible in real time. Not a screenshot, not an Excel sheet — a link. No link, no verification.
  2. Drawdown depth. Find the largest drawdown on the account. If it is 50–80%, one day you could be in it. If there are no drawdowns at all with high returns, losses are most likely just not being closed.
  3. A stop-loss on every trade. Open any ten trades on the monitor. Each one should have a stop-loss set in advance.
  4. Where the money is. Only in your own broker account, in your name, with you controlling withdrawals.
  5. How the trader gets paid. A share of profit only means they earn only when you do. Subscription fees and "joining fees" are a reason to be careful.
  6. Can you start on a demo. An honest trader has nothing to hide and will happily connect you to a demo account so you can watch the trading without money.
  7. What they say about risk. If the answer to "what losses can there be?" is "we don't have losses", that answers all your other questions.

  • Copy trading itself is not a scam: the schemes around it are.
  • The main red flags: sending money to the trader, guaranteed income, no live monitor, no stop-losses.
